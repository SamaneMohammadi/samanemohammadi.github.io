EFU — enforcing the right to be forgotten

Machine unlearning in federated learning, where the server coordinating training cannot be trusted with the deletion request.

Why it's hard
GDPR gives people the right to have their data deleted, but you cannot lift a row back out of a trained model, and retraining from scratch is infeasible. In federated learning it is worse: the party that would carry out the deletion is the same party you are trying to keep the request private from.
What I built
A framework using functional encryption that lets a client remove their data's influence from a collaboratively trained model. The server executes the unlearning without learning whose data was removed or what the request contained, so deletion does not depend on trusting it.
Why it matters beyond privacy
Removing a specific learned influence from a deployed model, verifiably and without retraining, is the same problem as removing a capability you no longer want a model to have.

Where bias lives inside an LLM

Locating the specific attention heads and feed-forward layers that carry a bias, then fine-tuning only those against a fairness-aware objective.

The premise
Bias is not spread evenly through a language model. It concentrates in identifiable components. If that is true, model-wide retraining is the wrong instrument — it is expensive and it damages capabilities you wanted to keep.
The approach
Localise the components that carry the behaviour, then apply a targeted fine-tune with fairness written into the loss. The intervention is narrow by construction, which is what keeps the capability cost down.
Status
In progress. The open questions are how cleanly bias localises across model families, and how much of the reduction survives further fine-tuning.

Auditing AI tools that screen job applicants

Whether LLM-based hiring systems are actually more robust and more equitable than the algorithmic methods they are replacing — and how you would measure it.

Three questions
What security and fairness failures arise across the hiring pipeline, and what do they cost. Whether LLM-based approaches genuinely improve on what came before, rather than being assumed to. And what an evaluation framework needs to contain so that any hiring system can be measured on both.
Why this domain
Hiring is a deployed, high-stakes, legally constrained use of LLMs where the harms are concrete and the incumbent systems are well studied. It is a good testbed for evaluation methodology that generalises past it.

EncCluster — encryption that scales with the model

Making functional and homomorphic encryption practical in federated learning, including on resource-constrained devices.

The obstacle
Encrypting model updates gives strong privacy guarantees and is usually dismissed as too expensive: cost scales with the number of parameters, which rules it out for anything large or anything running on a phone.
The idea
Compress client updates through weight clustering and probabilistic filters before encrypting, so that what gets encrypted is a compact representation rather than the full parameter set. Communication and computation costs fall, updates stay encrypted through aggregation, and accuracy holds.
PMCJ ’25 · NeurIPS ’24 workshop · ISPEC ’23 Paper Code Talk

Federated learning on hardware that actually differs

A physical testbed for training and deploying speech emotion recognition across heterogeneous edge devices, to measure efficiency, fairness, and privacy under real conditions.

Why build the hardware
Federated learning results are usually reported from simulation, where every client is equally fast and equally available. Device heterogeneity changes who contributes to the model and therefore who it works well for — a fairness effect that simulation hides.
What came out of it
An empirical account of the efficiency, fairness, and privacy trade-offs in asynchronous federated learning across real devices, plus the differential privacy work that preceded it.

About

I am finishing a PhD in the School of Informatics at the University of Edinburgh, supervised by Dr. Marc Juarez. Most of my work has been on privacy-preserving and distributed machine learning — unlearning, encryption, and the fairness costs that appear when you deploy any of it on real hardware.

I started the PhD at RISE Research Institutes of Sweden on EU consortium projects, which meant building things that had to survive contact with hospitals, industrial partners, and devices that do not behave like a simulator. That shaped what I find interesting: methods that hold up under constraints someone else imposed, not ones that only work in the setting where they were invented.

I am currently most interested in precise intervention on trained models — changing one property without disturbing the rest — and in the evaluation methods you need to tell whether the change actually worked. I am open to research roles from December 2026, and reachable at samaneh.mohammadi@ed.ac.uk.
